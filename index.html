Phoenix: Scaling Robotic Manipulation through World Modeling and Physical Interaction Learning

Code🤗Models

I. Introduction: Learning general capabilities from experience

The goal of Phoenix is to learn general robotic manipulation capabilities from large-scale experience and translate them into precise interaction skills in the physical world. To this end, we develop a unified system spanning data collection and processing, pretraining, post-training, and reinforcement learning. This system provides the basis for investigating how scaling data and compute can improve manipulation generalization in open environments and establish a transferable foundation for fine-grained interaction.

Phoenix-MWA is the foundation model of this system. It leverages dual-teacher distillation to combine geometric priors with temporal knowledge and a causal-semantic VAE to construct a compact visual latent space. Conditioned on observations and language instructions, the model jointly generates future visual states and actions, connecting the prediction of environmental dynamics with the learning of manipulation behavior. To facilitate reproducibility, Phoenix-MWA uses open-source datasets for pretraining. Its multi-source pretraining pool contains approximately 54,400 raw hours, approximately 39,300 effective hours, and roughly 3.38 billion raw frames. Under the corresponding task-specific post-training configurations, the model achieves success rates of 94.63% on LIBERO-Plus, 66.48% on LIBERO-Pro, 82.67% on RoboCasa-GR1, and 72.42% on RoboTwin 2.0 Clean2Random (C2R).

Moving from general manipulation to precise interaction, Phoenix-ILA focuses on the last centimeter of manipulation. It combines position, force, and touch into latent interaction units, using a small amount of post-training data to learn task-specific contact and control patterns. Phoenix-PhysCanvas takes a step further: an action-conditioned visual and tactile world-model simulator predicts how actions change the environment and its contact state, providing an interactive environment for policy reinforcement learning.

Phoenix unifies pretraining for general capabilities, post-training for task-specific skills, and reinforcement learning for improved execution, charting a path from large-scale data to precise physical interaction. We will open-source the complete data processing and annotation pipelines, together with the pretraining and post-training code and model weights.

II. Method

II.1 Phoenix-MWA: Learning perception, prediction, and action together

Built on DINOv3, the Phoenix Encoder uses feature alignment and masked prediction to combine geometric priors from VGGT with temporal knowledge from V-JEPA. A causal-semantic VAE compresses the resulting high-dimensional visual features into a compact latent space, using causal temporal encoding to preserve semantic and spatial structure while reducing redundancy and preventing leakage from future observations. Phoenix-MWA jointly predicts future states and actions in visual-dynamics and semantic latent spaces, connecting the evolution of the environment with manipulation behavior.

II.2 Phoenix-ILA: Learning the last centimeter of manipulation

Phoenix-ILA learns decodable interaction latents for contact-rich manipulation, jointly capturing motion and contact evolution. Conditioned on multi-view visual observations, an interaction encoder maps synchronized motion and tactile sequences into a compact latent representation. A time-conditioned decoder reconstructs motion, tactile deformation, and force proxies at queried times, preserving the temporal coordination between movement and contact over time. Policies learn to generate these interaction latents through conditional flow matching, and the shared decoder converts them into executable action sequences for precise physical interaction.

II.3 Phoenix-PhysCanvas: Improving policies inside a world model

Phoenix-PhysCanvas provides an interactive world-model simulator for policy refinement. It predicts visual and tactile feedback conditioned on robot actions, allowing Phoenix-MWA to act on the resulting observations in a closed loop. A reward model evaluates simulated interactions to guide reinforcement-learning post-training, refining manipulation skills before deployment in the physical world.

III. Pretraining data

III.1 Pretraining data distribution

To facilitate reproducibility, Phoenix-MWA uses open-source datasets for pretraining. This diverse pretraining pool spans robot demonstrations, egocentric human videos, and UMI recordings. The collection contains 54,413 hours of raw video, totaling approximately 3.38 billion frames, with 39,306 hours retained after processing. Robot data accounts for 31.09% of the effective duration, while embodiment-free data contributes 68.91%, comprising egocentric videos (63.58%) and UMI recordings (5.33%). This combination brings together robot interaction experience and broader human manipulation observations for large-scale pretraining.

III.2 Pretraining data processing pipeline

Processing standardizes formats, selects subsets, filters invalid records, and isolates anomalous labels. We trim redundant stationary segments at episode boundaries and retain windows that satisfy temporal continuity and task-boundary constraints. Ego data receives script-level language annotations; robot data is aligned in coordinates, units, action timing, and valid dimensions. During training, we sample alternative language expressions while preserving task and action semantics. We will open-source the complete data processing and annotation pipelines to make these steps reproducible.

III.3 Action space design

We use a unified 80-dimensional state and action space.

Fixed semantic slots cover the left and right arms, end effectors, and grippers, with additional channels for dexterous hands, the torso, and the mobile base. Single-arm data maps to the right-arm slots. Missing or invalid dimensions are zero-filled and excluded from the loss through per-timestep, per-dimension masks.

VI. Analysis

We examine how pretraining changes representations and video prediction, and how the broader system supports precise skills and policy post-training.

VI.1 What changes in representation, prediction, and contact?

To assess whether pretraining yields a more balanced visual feature spectrum, we sample 10,000 frames each from the pretraining validation set and LIBERO-Plus, using identical frames for all three encoders. Singular value decomposition of the centered CLS and Patch Mean feature matrices gives the effective rank, measuring how evenly feature variation is distributed across principal directions.

A higher rank indicates more evenly distributed variation, but does not by itself establish better control performance.
